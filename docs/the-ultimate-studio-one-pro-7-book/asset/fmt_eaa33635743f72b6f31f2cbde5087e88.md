Groove quantization generally works best when it’s applied to all the tracks in a project. If some instruments are quantized to a groove whereas others are quantized to the grid, their rhythms will be inconsistent. In order to maintain a coherent arrangement, make sure to stick to either one of the grid or groove quantization methods.

In this section, we took an in-depth look at several methods to fix timing errors in audio recordings. Now, we are ready to tackle a slightly more complicated task – working with Melodyne to enhance vocal recordings.

# Using Melodyne to fix and enhance vocal recordings

**Melodyne** is an industry-leading software tool that allows you to manipulate the pitch and timing of individual notes in an audio recording. Melodyne is developed by **Celemony**. Although Celemony is not affiliated with PreSonus, they have worked in close cooperation for more than a decade now to forge an unmatched level of integration between Melodyne and Studio One. **Melodyne Essential**, the most lightweight version of the software, is bundled with Studio One. The capabilities provided by Melodyne Essential are sufficient for the needs of most day-to-day music production.

### Important note

Although Melodyne is capable of manipulating the timing of audio, we will not cover that functionality in this book. Over the years, I have found Studio One’s own time manipulation capabilities, which we discussed in the preceding section of this chapter, to be highly effective and sufficient. Therefore, coverage of Melodyne here will be confined to manipulating pitch in an audio recording.

Now, let’s talk about how to use Melodyne. First, you will learn how to correct out-of-tune vocal performances. Then, we will explore creative ways of using Melodyne to add background harmony vocals to your project.

## Fixing an out-of-tune vocal performance with Melodyne

Every note in music has a predetermined frequency, established by international standards. When a vocalist sings a note, we expect it to be at the frequency specified for that particular note. If the singer delivers a frequency that is higher than it should be, the note is sharp. If the frequency is lower than it should be, the note is flat.

**Pitch correction** software and hardware have been widely used for decades to detect these deviations and shift the frequency of a note back to where it’s supposed to be, either automatically or manually. This is a complex procedure that potentially results in severe artifacts in audio (think auto-tune). Melodyne’s strength lies in its ability to minimize these artifacts and deliver pristine, natural-sounding results.

Now, let’s take a look at how you can use Melodyne to fix out-of-tune notes. Find a vocal recording that you’d like to work on. On the `Arrangement` window, right-click on the event. From the pop-up menu, go to `Audio`, and click `Edit with Melodyne`. This will open Melodyne in the `Edit` window:

![Figure 8.13: Melodyne Essential in the Edit window](image/B31512_08_13.jpg)

Figure 8.13: Melodyne Essential in the Edit window

The blobs in the middle of the interface are visual representations of each note in the vocal recording. The white and gray stripes that extend across this window correspond to musical notes, listed on the column on the far left. The numbers above the window correspond to the bar numbers in your Studio One project.

Use the navigation bars at the bottom and far right of the window to move around. These navigation bars also serve double duty as zoom controls – hover your mouse on either end and the cursor will turn into a magnifier icon. You can then drag your mouse to zoom in and out as you like.

Now that you’re familiar with Melodyne’s interface, you can start working with it. The first thing you need to do is go to `Algorithm` on Melodyne’s own top menu and make sure that `Melodic (Standard)` is selected. This ensures that Melodyne uses the correct algorithm for pitch detection.

Let’s take a closer look at blobs and learn how to work with them. Here’s an example:

![Figure 8.14: A blob representing a slightly flat note](image/B31512_08_14.jpg)

Figure 8.14: A blob representing a slightly flat note

The wiggly line inside the blob represents little fluctuations in the singer’s frequency as they sang the note. The blob itself represents the average of all these fluctuations. Here, the vocalist wanted to sing the *A* note but was a bit flat. We can tell because the blob shifts downward toward the *G#* note, instead of landing squarely on the *A* note. You can easily correct this by dragging the blob up. It will snap to the center of the nearest note, which, in this case, is *A*. Here is the same note in *Figure 8.14* after it has been corrected:

![Figure 8.15: A blob representing a pitch-corrected note](image/B31512_08_15.jpg)

Figure 8.15: A blob representing a pitch-corrected note

If you want to disable this automatic snapping option temporarily, hold down the `Alt`/`Option` key while dragging. This will allow you to move the blob up or down with a finer resolution.

One thing to watch out for when manually correcting notes this way is a singing technique called *glissando*:

![Figure 8.16: Several notes connected with glissando](image/B31512_08_16.jpg)

Figure 8.16: Several notes connected with glissando

**Glissando** is when a singer or an instrument player slides into a note, rather than landing on it directly. In *Figure 8.16*, all the notes are connected with glissandos, as you can see from the lines connecting the blobs. While it’s a cool vocal technique, it presents two challenges for pitch correction – first, it tricks the algorithm when calculating the average frequency of a note, and second, trying to pitch-correct a glissando defeats the purpose of singing with a glissando in the first place, resulting in the famous auto-tune effect used as a creative device in certain music genres.

Whenever you see a glissando similar to those in *Figure 8.16*, detach it from the main body of the blob. To do that, hover your mouse on the upper half of the blob. It will turn into the `Note Separation` tool. Double-click with this tool to divide the blob. Here is the same note after I separated the glissandos at the note’s beginning and end:

![Figure 8.17: The same note with glissandos detached](image/B31512_08_17.jpg)

Figure 8.17: The same note with glissandos detached

Note that by separating the glissandos, I forced the algorithm to recalculate the position of the blob, and it’s now sharp in pitch, leaning toward the *F* note. Now, when I drag this blob to its correct position, the algorithm will do a much better job of pitch correction, as it no longer takes the glissandos into account.
