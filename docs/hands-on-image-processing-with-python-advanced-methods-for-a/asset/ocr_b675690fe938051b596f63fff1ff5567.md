The U-Net estimates the noise component, while the scheduler computes the next denoised sample $x_{t-1}$. Conceptually, each iteration performs: Noisy Image → Less Noisy Image → Cleaner Image until a realistic image emerges.

The above visualization highlights one of the most intuitive aspects of diffusion models: image generation is literally a process of progressively removing noise (notice how visual structure gradually emerges during denoising).

$DDPMs$ provide a fundamentally different approach to image generation than GANs. Instead of learning a direct mapping from latent vectors to images, they learn a sequence of denoising operations that gradually transform random noise into realistic samples. Their training objective is stable, their latent representations are highly expressive, and they consistently achieve state-of-the-art image quality.

The forward diffusion process progressively corrupts an image according to:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon,$$

while the reverse diffusion process learns to estimate and remove the noise component $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$, thereby reconstructing images from pure noise. This simple yet powerful idea forms the foundation of modern diffusion-based generative AI systems.

# Latent Diffusion Models

The original **Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)** perform the diffusion process directly on image pixels. Although highly effective, pixel-space diffusion is computationally expensive because high-resolution images contain millions of values. **Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs)** address this limitation by performing diffusion in a compressed latent representation learned by a **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)** rather than in pixel space. This idea forms the foundation of modern diffusion systems such as Stable Diffusion.
