
This approach can be very effective. Different agents can focus on what they do best, and the system can iterate more quickly across multiple steps. The agents often have different capabilities or thinking levels, or may even be from different companies. For example, some advanced developers have found that OpenAI’s Codex at very high thinking level is best for planning, and then they hand off the implementation to Claude Code Sonnet, or a local model like Qwen3-Coder-Next or DeepSeek Coder. In [* Chapter 11 *](#11.xhtml#_idTextAnchor764), * Setting Up Your Project Environment *, I cover local models in more detail.

## Why agents matter

At first glance, multi-agent systems sound like a clear upgrade. In many cases, they are. They can handle larger, more complex tasks, work across multiple files and layers of a project, and catch issues earlier through built-in review or testing steps.

But there is a cost that is easy to overlook.

Every time an agent performs a step, it sends and receives data. In a multi-agent system, this happens multiple times per task, and often between several agents. This has a direct impact on token usage. If multiple agents are communicating with each other, the same context may be passed around several times. This can increase token usage quickly, even for tasks that seem small.

This is why developers often notice that agent-based tools feel more expensive or burn through quota faster than simple chat-based tools.

For learning, it is important to use these tools with some discipline.

If you rely on a multi-agent system to build large features from scratch, it may produce working code, but you risk paying for a lot of computation that does not actually help you learn.

A better approach is to:

* Use smaller, focused tasks rather than large open-ended goals
* Limit how much the agent is allowed to do in one go
* Review each step instead of accepting everything automatically
* Be aware that more automation usually means higher token usage

As you gain experience, you can start to take advantage of multi-agent workflows for more complex tasks. At that point, the trade-offs between speed, cost, and understanding will become clearer.

## Practical tips for collaborating with any AI

Here’s how to make these tools actual teachers instead of glorified code generators.

* **State your learning goal up front**: Instead of “write me a Django view”, ask “teach me how Django views handle GET vs POST with examples.” That forces the AI to explain, not just produce an end result.
* **Ask for step-by-step explanations**: Always include phrasing like “explain line by line and why it matters.”
* **Iterate with small prompts**: Instead of huge requests, break things down into smaller steps. You can always use follow-ups like “now expand on this” when you want to go deeper.
* **Use editor integration for practice**: Chatbots inside editors like Copilot, Gemini, and DeepSeek make learning contextual from the active project and its code files. You’re not just learning abstract theory; the chatbot is applying it to your current code.
* **Validate with multiple sources.** For tricky concepts, ask two assistants (for example, ChatGPT and Claude) and compare answers. Discrepancies often reveal subtle nuances you’d miss otherwise.

A useful prompt is the following:

```
Act as a strategic advisor. Based on my goal below, recommend ONE best option and explain why it is superior to alternatives. Then, list two backup options and specify exactly when I should choose them instead. My goal is [Insert your goal here]
```

# Pros and cons of AI tools

Many people claim that human developers will soon be obsolete because AI will write software autonomously and make coders unnecessary. But I am unconvinced that we have entered anything resembling a post-developer world.

## Companies are increasingly using AI tools

It is true that companies are increasingly adopting AI tools. For example, an article in Forbes reported that AI writes over 25% of the code committed at Google. But human developers remain deeply involved in the process, guiding AI, reviewing its output, editing the code, and shaping it into a coherent product. AI is a tool controlled by humans, not a replacement for humans.

## Human + AI = success

While AI tools like GitHub Copilot can produce impressive results in well-defined tasks, they still require a human hand on the tiller. To mix my metaphors, using AI in development is like flying a plane with cruise control: the aircraft goes where you point it, but without constant guidance and corrections from a skilled pilot, it could drift off course, and critical steps in the flight like landing and takeoff should be done entirely under human control.

As a software developer using AI tools, if you do not understand the code being generated, you won’t know when or how to correct it. This limitation is central to why the idea that we are entering a post-human programmer world is exaggerated.

## Limits of AI

There are plenty of tasks where current large language models struggle. Some of the most hyped tools have failed in real testing contexts. You might find that your AI tool can only complete a subset of assignments satisfactorily and you ultimately spend more time working around its mistakes than you would have writing the code yourself.

These results suggest that AI in its current form is good at well-scoped, repetitive tasks, but poor at open-ended or complex development problems without significant human intervention.

There has also been interesting recent research suggesting a gap between perceived and actual productivity gains from AI assistance. A 2025 randomized controlled trial by the nonprofit research group **METR** (**Model Evaluation & Threat Research**) reported that developers using AI tools actually took longer to complete tasks compared to those who did not use AI, even while they felt like they were faster. This speaks to the psychological allure of AI promises versus the messy reality of real engineering work.

Researchers found developers spent extra time prompting the AI, reviewing suggestions, correcting errors, and integrating generated code.

In other words: less typing, more verification. Developers had to verify and correct AI suggestions that were often only broadly accurate.

The key paper is *Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity* by researchers at METR. The main finding was that developers using AI tools took 19% longer to complete tasks than those who did not. Yet they believed AI made them faster by about 20–24%. You can read more at the following link: [https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)

This study does *not* mean AI always slows developers down. It applies mainly to experienced developers with familiar codebases and complex real-world tasks.

Other studies show the opposite.
