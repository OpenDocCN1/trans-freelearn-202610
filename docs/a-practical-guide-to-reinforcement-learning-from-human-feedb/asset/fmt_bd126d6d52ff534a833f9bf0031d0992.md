
## Reward Model Evaluation

### Rank Correlation Metrics

When the dataset has multiple responses compared with ranking based scale, this metric is more appropriate as it measures the degree to which the two rankings agree. It measures how well overall rank ordering predicted by `RM` aligns with what humans might have ranked. For example, let us consider the case where instead of pairwise ranking, humans score multiple responses in order of preference e.g. `response3` is best, followed by `response 5`, then `response 2`. The rank correlation computation involves following steps: First, the responses are ranked by the `RM` e.g. `` `R(response1)` ``, `` `R(response2)` ``, … This is followed by sorting the scores by their score values, to get the `RM`’s ranking. Then, the `RM`’s ranking is compared to human ranking. The comparison of `RM` with human ranking can use `Spearman’s` rank correlation coefficient, which uses the ranked positions and computes the standard correlation coefficient `ρ`, where `` `cov` `` is the covariance between `RM` score and human preference, and the metric ranges between `-1` and `1`. Similarly, another metric comparing rank ordering, `Kendall’s` tau, can also be computed. It measures the strength of association, and in simplified form can be represented as `τ`.

### RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) and MAE (Mean Absolute Error)

Depending upon the subject domain and response types, some applications might require scores to be scalar values ranging between `0-100` or `0-1`, or even a likert-scale `1-5`. In such cases, standard regression metrics such as `Root Mean Squared Error` (`RSME`) or `mean absolute error` (`MAE`) can be used. These scores provide standard quality scores, and are good for determining magnitude of closeness. While these metrics are good for ensuring model training best practices are followed, before using in real-world applications, additional generalization tests should be conducted. Some of these tests can be as follows.

### Out-of-distribution Robustness

Out-of-distribution robustness is mainly a test of model generalization, to test whether the model can perform well on data that has not been part of distribution of the training data. This requires collecting data, which is dissimilar to training data, and testing how model scores compare to human preferences. If model scores disagree with human preferences, all the systematic differences can be observed and, in those examples, feature engineering-based inputs should be collected for either retraining or fine-tuning of `RM`. While this step can be exhaustive, it is a necessary step for real world applications. The best practice is to use the model in real-world and test for out-of-distribution robustness, collect the data, retrain or fine-tune on it, and then re-test. This can be a periodic repetitive process.

## Policy Performance

No matter how well the `RM` performs, its eventual purpose is to help train policy that will align with human intent for the language model. Therefore, the policy performance can be a practical metric for testing whether `RM` is useful. It is similar to the integration tests performed for software modules. Good policy performance however, does not guarantee that the `RM` is robust. It is only an indirect indication that given `RM` is directionally correct. The converse, a good `RM` does not guarantee better performance either, and often moderate accuracy is enough, as per recent findings in this paper, *The Accuracy Paradox in RLHF: When Better Reward Models Don’t Yield Better Language Models*: [https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.174.pdf](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.174.pdf). The `RM` might not be a perfect reflection of human preferences in all scenarios, but generally it is providing enough signal to push the policy toward behaviors that humans generally like. It is totally possible that it has captured partial correlations and is useful for the tests or examples used for testing policy performance. Therefore, in practice it is not sufficient.

### Indicators of Poor RM-Induced Policy Behavior

Apart from the evaluation metrics we discussed in the last section, there are some indicators of policy behavior resulting from poor reward model accuracy. Some of them are as follows:

*   **Preference Misalignment:** A fine-tuned policy may generate outputs that humans do not actually prefer, despite the reward model showing high accuracy on its validation set. This suggests that the `RM` overfits superficial patterns in the preference data (e.g., length, sentiment) rather than capturing deeper intent.
*   **Reward Hacking:** Policies often learn to game the reward model. Common symptoms include overly verbose completions, excessive hedging by adding "As an AI language model..." to start a sentence, or moralizing statements designed to trigger high reward scores. This issue frequently arises from deficiencies in the reward model, which stem from human labelers typically assigning higher ratings to longer texts.
*   **Behavior Collapse or Mode Narrowing:** Poor reward shaping may cause the policy to generate overly similar or conservative outputs, hurting diversity, informativeness, or creativity. This is especially problematic in open-ended tasks like summarization or dialogue.

### Strategies to Address Poor Policy Performance

While poor `RM` performance can be fixed using supervised learning strategies similar to traditional ML approaches such as data balancing, regularization, and out-of-distribution effect (discussed in previous section), the metrics do not capture some of their performance issues affecting policy behavior. When poor policy performance reveals `RM` issues, several strategies can help:

*   **Refine and expand preference data:** Target areas where the policy underperforms. Collect more comparisons involving current policy outputs, especially failure cases.
*   **Apply active relabeling:** Sample uncertain, low-confidence, or controversial completions for fresh labeling. This helps improve the reward model where it lacks clear guidance.
*   **Clip or normalize rewards:** Prevent extreme reward values from destabilizing policy updates. Helps mitigate reward hacking.
*   **Use ensemble reward models:** Reduces overfitting to specific annotator quirks or biases, and improves generalization across prompt types.
*   **Evaluate with policy probing checkpoints:** Use intermediate policies to test reward model robustness over the course of fine-tuning.

In practice, the ultimate test for `RM` is how the final policy trained—fine-tuned language model based on `RM`—behaves. Besides, `RM` accuracy and impact of policy behavior, the policy training also contributes to how the model responses are; we will cover these in the next chapter.
