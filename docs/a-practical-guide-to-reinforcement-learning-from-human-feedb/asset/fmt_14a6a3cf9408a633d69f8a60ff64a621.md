
# Leveraging Domain Knowledge in Reinforcement Learning

Leveraging domain knowledge in `reinforcement learning` (`RL`) can significantly enhance performance and decision-making by providing a structured framework for understanding the problem space and guiding the learning process. Domain knowledge refers to expertise and insights specific to the problem domain, which can come from various sources such as subject matter experts, prior research, or practical experience.

## Problem Formulation

Domain knowledge helps in framing the `RL` problem by identifying relevant states, actions, and rewards, thus reducing the search space and making the learning process more efficient. For example, in robotics, understanding the dynamics of the environment and the physical constraints of the robot can help design more effective state representations and action spaces. The list below explains how domain knowledge can help define each of the components in `RL` framework.

*   **Defining states**: Including the important states as part of the agent observation is critical to the training efficiency of the agent. Too many unnecessary states can add noise and overhead, and unnecessarily higher capacity requirement to a function approximator such as a neural network can slow training, or worse—learn some unnecessary correlations. On the contrary, missing some important states can lead to agent having to learn and model some additional relationships between states that could have been simply specified. In case of a robot (e.g. a robot arm or a mobile robot), not providing velocities, and only providing position information can lead to inefficient learning.

*   **Defining actions**: Subject matter expertise can help define what actions can be available and how they are constrained. For example, a robot arm may move only in a particular range of angles, and subject matter expert knowledge on constraints on the action defines action space.

*   **Defining rewards**: In complex industrial scenarios, the region of desirable and undesirable state-action space may not be obvious to lay person, and subject matter experts can help specify these regions, and shape the reward functions more accurately.

*   **Episode lengths and termination criteria**: Human expertise can also help determine when a particular trajectory should be terminated, and episode ended, so that agent can abort a trajectory navigating in an unsafe region. This can avoid areas of exploration that are not useful to learn, in turn making training more efficient.

Note that all the above incorporation of human expertise can be useful in conventional `RL` applications. In `RLHF`, evaluative feedback rewards from experts can be incorporated in addition to defining the original reward from environment. `RLHF` uses human feedback to train AI systems to appear more human-like. In `RLHF`, human feedback is used to evaluate the quality of the agent's responses or actions and guide the learning process. The goal of `RLHF` is to make the AI system more accurate and human-like in its responses. The key difference between the two approaches is the role of human expertise. In incorporating human expertise in `RL`, the expertise is used to define the problem and guide the learning process, whereas in `RLHF`, human feedback is used to evaluate and improve the agent's performance. In other words, incorporating human expertise in `RL` is about defining the problem and setting the goals, while `RLHF` is about providing feedback to achieve those goals.

To illustrate the difference, consider a robotics example. In incorporating human expertise in `RL`, a subject matter expert might define the states, actions, and rewards for a robot arm, such as the joint angles, motor speeds, and distance to target. In `RLHF`, a human might provide feedback on the quality of the robot's movements, such as rating the smoothness or accuracy of the arm's motion, and this feedback would be used to update the reward function and improve the robot's performance. In practice, both approaches can be combined. Considering the robot arm example, experts can define states, actions and rewards, and collect robot motion trajectories that can be used for rating or scoring using human feedback. Based on this feedback on the quality of robot's trajectories, agent training can be refined by building a reward model. To deal with dynamic environments and changes, human expertise can be incorporated in design and algorithms to counter changing environmental factors, by training adaptive `RL` systems. Human feedback can further help with refining policy decisions during unforeseen scenarios or real-time situations emerging in the environment. We will now discuss the types of human feedback used in `RL`, in order to refine our understanding further.

## Types of Human Feedback in `RL`

The idea of incorporating human feedback into `RL` has been tried in several different ways in the past, and has gained traction in recent years, especially with the emergence of large language models and training of foundation models. Use of human signals for training agents has multiple forms. Broadly, human demonstration and human feedback are classified as different categories of using human signals for training agents. In `RL`, reward function design and engineering can be subjective, and getting it right can be tricky. Using human demonstration to define reward function is studied and practiced in the subdomain of `inverse RL`. Similarly, supervised learning using human demonstration and a direct mapping from states to actions based on the demonstration, is called `behavioral cloning`, which can be beneficial when defining reward functions is hard even for experts e.g. for complex environments. Both, `inverse RL` and `behavioral cloning` are methods within the class of `learning from demonstration` (`LfD`). `Imitation learning` is similar to `LfD` and includes `behavioral cloning` and `inverse RL`, but it can also extend to learning from any form of expert behavior, whether provided explicitly through demonstrations or implicitly through interactions. While `LfD` primarily focuses on direct demonstrations, it can also incorporate learning from the consequences of actions to refine the learned behavior. Therefore, both `LfD` and `IL` can involve learning from the consequences of actions, and the distinction between them lies more in their scope and methods rather than the type of feedback used.

This brings us to incorporating human feedback, which aims at minimizing the subjectivity and effort in reward design and engineering. Human feedback approach in reinforcement learning has evolved over the last two decades. The current usage of reinforcement learning from human feedback encompasses several methods, which can be broadly categorized as below:

*   **Learning from Human Reward or Evaluative Reinforcement**: Direct evaluative feedback impacting the agent's rewards.

*   **Preference-Based RL**: Feedback in the form of preferences between actions or outcomes to guide policy development.

*   **Human Feedback for Defining Reward Models**: Using human feedback to construct or refine a reward model.

*   **Semi-Supervised RL with Absolute Feedback on a Subset of Experiences**: Combining supervised learning with `RL` through feedback on specific experiences.
