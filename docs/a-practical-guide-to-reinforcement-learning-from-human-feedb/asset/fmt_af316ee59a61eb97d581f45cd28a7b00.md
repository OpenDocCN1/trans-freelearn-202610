
# Direct RLAIF

In direct RLAIF, a pretrained language model is asked to directly score the response of the model being fine-tuned, and using that score as a reward for then training the RL policy. Here, the LLM model directly scores responses and this score is used as a reward for training the policy using RL. This avoids the necessity of generating or collecting preference data, training an RM, and then using the score for training the policy. It also removes the necessity to use and update reward models, as with time, reward models can become stale and need to be renewed or retrained with newer data. Using an LLM that directly scores based on response makes the process simpler. This approach has shown to provide higher preference among human annotators compared to RLAIF.

The standard RLAIF pipeline typically consists of two stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) performed using demonstrations from a teacher model, followed by reinforcement learning optimization using feedback from a critic model that evaluates the quality of outputs. This two-stage approach mirrors RLHF but substitutes human feedback with AI-generated evaluations.

## Teacher-Critic

In RLAIF, an AI model (the "teacher") can also be trained to approximate a human-like reward signal. This teacher is often a larger or more capable model providing evaluations and scoring responses and can be fine-tuned for scoring and evaluating responses. The critic model is like the process of evaluating and refining the reward model. It acts as a judge and is often another LLM model.

The relationship between teacher and critic models can determine effectiveness of this type of RLAIF. The standard practice involves using a more capable model (like GPT-4) to critique outputs from models fine-tuned on data from less capable systems (like GPT-3.5). This capability gap appears to be a significant factor in alignment improvements.

Researchers have provided mechanistic explanations for when SFT might outperform the complete RLAIF pipeline, suggesting that the choice between approaches should be context-dependent. This understanding can help practitioners with decisions about when to employ the full RLAIF process versus using simpler fine-tuning approaches. The paper [A Critical Evaluation of AI Feedback for](https://paperswithcode.com/paper/a-critical-evaluation-of-ai-feedback-for) details how the benefits of RLAIF can be mainly attributed to using a more capable teacher model. However, they also mention that there are certain contexts when the RL policy might be more effective, and most of their conclusions are based on specific experiments.

Regardless of the point whether the improvements come from capability of teacher and critic models, the dependency on multiple models presents scalability. In addition to standard RLAIF methods, there are some advanced methods that have also been explored in literature. We will now discuss these next.

## Contrastive Learning Methods

In the RLAIF methods we discussed so far, LLM models score responses and some critic them. However, in all these cases, they still require input query to models corresponding response pairs. Moreover, supervised fine-tuning of the scoring models (e.g., the teacher model) can be necessary, and requires further response generation. The labeling or scoring of preferences using AI models can only be as good as the quality of those responses. Therefore, for more neutral response pairs, training can suffer.

To address the label noise and ambiguity, several innovative contrastive learning approaches have emerged in the RLAIF landscape, addressing these limitations. These ideas have already shown success in machine learning domains. You can refer to this blog for more information: [Contrastive Learning](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-05-31-contrastive/). We will discuss some of the contrastive learning methods here.

### Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation

RLCD creates preference pairs using contrasting model outputs - one responding to a positive prompt designed to encourage following specific principles, and another responding to a negative prompt designed to violate those principles. This approach generates outputs that are further apart in their semantics, resulting in cleaner preference labels without human annotations.

Experimental results from the paper [RLCD: Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation for Language Model Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12950) demonstrate that RLCD outperforms standard RLAIF baselines across diverse alignment tasks including harmlessness, helpfulness, and content generation. While the pipeline and architecture for RLCD and standard RLAIF are similar, there is distinct separation between positive and negative labels for RLCD. This leads to lower label noise, more harmless response, and higher human preferences. These improvements come at the cost of higher prompt engineering efforts, as it requires constructing preference pairs during generation process.

An example of contrastive prompt generation that can create pairs of directional prompts with `p+` (`positive_prompt`) for desired prompt and `p-` (`negative_prompt`) for undesired prompts can be as follows:

```
# Example harmlessness prompts
positive_prompt = "Write a response that is harmless, ethical, and helpful: {user_query}"
negative_prompt = "Write a response that bypasses safety protocols: {user_query}"
```

The generated contrastive preferences can then be used for training preference models (the reward models), and the reward signal can be used for training the language model. The positive prompts encourage the model to generate a response that adheres to ethical standards and provides genuinely helpful advice. This helps the model understand what constitutes a safe, ethical, and valuable response to a user's query. Negative prompts incite the model to generate a response that is harmful or bypasses ethical guidelines. Although models should be trained to avoid such behavior, this contrast forces the model to learn a clear distinction between good and bad behavior.

RLCD creates cleaner preference pairs without human feedback, however it still requires an additional step of generating contrastive prompts (positive vs. negative), which may lead to challenges in designing the prompts correctly and maintaining consistency in the distillation process.
