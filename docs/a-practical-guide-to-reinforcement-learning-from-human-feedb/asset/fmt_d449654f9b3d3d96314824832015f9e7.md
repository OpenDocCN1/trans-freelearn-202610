`Cohen's kappa` helps determine whether data is balanced or unbalanced, and is higher for balanced data. However, it must be used in conjunction with accuracy metric, as it does not say much about accuracy. This metric can be further extended to scale to multiple raters, and the extended version is called `Fleiss' kappa`.

`Quadratic Weighted Kappa` is a variant of `Cohen's kappa` designed for **ordinal data**—ratings that follow a natural order (e.g., 1 < 2 < 3 < 4 < 5). It applies a weighting scheme that penalizes disagreements more as the distance between the assigned ratings grows. For instance, disagreeing on “1” vs. “5” is a bigger error than disagreeing on “3” vs. “4.”

`Fleiss' kappa` is a statistical measure of inter-rater reliability that generalizes `Cohen's kappa` to **more than two** raters. The formulation for `Fleiss's kappa` is similar to that of `Cohen's kappa`, but calculation of observed agreement and chance agreement are modified to account for multiple raters: `p_o` is mean of the agreement probability over all raters, and `p_e` is mean agreement probability over all raters if they were randomly assigned.

`Krippendorff's alpha` is a more general measure of reliability that can handle different data types (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) and different numbers of annotators. It also accounts for missing data, which often arises in large-scale annotation projects.

## Reference: Artstein and Poesio (2008)

Artstein and Poesio's *Inter-Coder Agreement for Computational Linguistics* (2008) is a comprehensive survey on measuring and interpreting inter-coder agreement in computational linguistics. They discuss:

- **Different agreement measures** (including kappa statistics and alpha coefficients).
- **When to use which measure** depending on the nature of the data (nominal vs. ordinal vs. interval).
- **Pitfalls and best practices**: how to design annotation tasks to reduce confusion, how to handle chance correction, and how reliability relates to validity.

If you are interested in more depth, you can refer to the online PDF here: [https://direct.mit.edu/coli/article/34/4/555/1999/Inter-Coder-Agreement-for-Computational](https://direct.mit.edu/coli/article/34/4/555/1999/Inter-Coder-Agreement-for-Computational) or here: [https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004.pdf](https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004.pdf).

Ensuring that annotators understand the definitions and instructions—and that they consistently apply them—is crucial. Artstein and Poesio's guidelines ([https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004.pdf](https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004.pdf)) can help pinpoint which reliability measure is most appropriate for your RLHF setting and how to interpret it.

# Other Considerations for Reward Modeling

Reward modeling is supervised learning from data, where preprocessed data has prompt input and responses from model as inputs, and preference ratings from annotators as reward score output. While quality of data is an important factor in how well the RM aligns with human preferences, there are some other considerations for training a well aligned model.

## Context and Chat History in Reward Modeling

The response of a model when a single input is provided can be different compared to the response as part of a multi-turn conversation. The additional information based on conversational history can help the model respond in alignment to the topic of conversation. The process of assessing data quality, especially the metrics, will not change much though. The annotators can rate response for the entire conversation or for the entire set of responses for that conversation session. It can also be rating each response and using cumulative score.

## Dealing with Underspecification and Overoptimization

RMs are inherently underspecified. They perform well when data is in-distribution of the training data, but the outputs can be unexpected when there is a distribution shift. Underspecification of RMs occurs because RMs are based on training using human feedback signals, and human feedback is only a proxy signal of thorough human evaluation. This results in overoptimization, where the model is aligned with human feedback that is not specifying the desired outcome accurately. Using an ensemble of the signals from multiple RMs can mitigate overoptimization as shown in [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.09244](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.09244). There are also other approaches of mitigating overoptimization, as in [https://openreview.net/pdf?id=gkfUvn0fLU](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=gkfUvn0fLU), which tries to show that constraining reinforcement learning can help with mitigating overoptimization.

## Evaluation Steps and Metrics

Some evaluation approaches can be extension of the evaluation passed to `RewardTrainer`. These can be in the form of held-out human comparison with following steps:

1. Collect a test data set similar to training set or use part of training data excluded from training.
2. Have human raters rate or rank or add preferences to each of the input and the pair of responses presented (in case of pairwise preference scoring).
3. Then compare the RM's predicted score with human rating.

In order to compare how well the RM predicted with respect to human rating, multiple scores can be used simultaneously.
