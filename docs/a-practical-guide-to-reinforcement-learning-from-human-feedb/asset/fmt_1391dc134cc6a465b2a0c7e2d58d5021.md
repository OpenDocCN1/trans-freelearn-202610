# Human Evaluation

Human evaluation remains the gold standard for assessing models trained via `RLHF` and related alignment methods like `DPO` and `RLAIF`. While increasingly augmented by automated tools, human judgment continues to be essential for evaluating nuanced aspects such as helpfulness, relevance, and value alignment. However, this process is complex, expensive, and prone to subjectivity.

## Crowdsourcing vs Expert Annotation

Most `RLHF` pipelines rely on crowdsourced feedback, wherein annotators are presented with paired model responses and asked to select the preferred output. This approach enables scalability across large datasets and diverse domains but introduces noise due to annotator inconsistency and limited domain expertise. In contrast, expert annotation is critical for complex or high-stakes domains such as legal reasoning, code generation, or medical advice. Experts can provide more reliable feedback but at significantly higher cost and reduced throughput. Hybrid pipelines sometimes use crowdworkers for general content and reserve experts for verification or adjudication stages. For example, OpenAI's `RLHF` pipeline for `InstructGPT` initially used crowdworkers for general tasks and later refined labels using expert feedback on more technical or sensitive queries.

The example below illustrates what an actual `RLHF` preference annotation looks like in practice, and how surface-level judgments (fluency, familiarity) often dominate.

```
Prompt: "How do I train for a marathon in 3 months?"
Response A: "Start by increasing mileage weekly and include one long run per week."
Response B: "Running is easy. Just go faster every week until race day."
Crowdworker label: A preferred (90% vote)
```

It is not just the expertise of annotators that impacts evaluation; the state of annotators and ability to comply also affect their rating, as we discuss next.

## Annotator Fatigue and Bias

Human raters often engage in repetitive tasks, leading to **fatigue**, which degrades annotation quality over time. This can manifest as rushed judgments, increased reliance on surface heuristics (e.g., fluency bias), or failure to spot subtle factual errors. Bias is another critical concern. Annotators bring cultural, linguistic, and cognitive biases that can skew results. If the annotator pool is not demographically and ideologically diverse, models may reinforce narrow worldviews or omit minority perspectives.

Mitigation strategies include:

- Randomizing response presentation order
- Monitoring annotator consistency
- Periodic calibration tasks with gold standard answers
- Diversity audits of annotator pools

### Inter-Annotator Agreement

Because human evaluation tasks often involve subjective interpretation (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness), inter-annotator agreement is a key quality control signal. High variance among raters may indicate poorly defined guidelines or inherently ambiguous tasks. The following table shows how ratings of annotators differ.

## Table 11.3: Agreement Table for Summary Ratings

| **Annotator** | **Rating** | **Reason** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 5 | "Clear and comprehensive." |
| B | 2 | "Omitted key political context." |

Statistical measures such as **Cohen's κ** or **Krippendorff's α** quantify agreement beyond chance. Low scores may indicate ambiguous prompts or insufficient annotation guidelines. In these cases, examples should be flagged for expert review or excluded from training.

### Disagreement in Summarization

In a human evaluation task for news summarization, annotators were asked to rank model-generated summaries based on overall quality. While some summaries were clearly better—fluent, factual, and concise—others provoked strong disagreement.

```
Prompt: "Summarize the article about the recent election debate."
Model Summary A:
"The debate focused on economic policy, healthcare, and climate change."
Model Summary B:
"The debate revealed deep divides on immigration, and the incumbent was heavily criticized."
```

To see how annotators interpreted these summaries differently, Table 11.4 shows their preferences and rationale.

## Table 11.4: Annotator Preferences

| Annotator | Preferred Summary | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Summary A | Covers major topics clearly and briefly |
| B | Summary B | Mentions critical tone missing in A |

This case illustrated several challenges:

- The **subjectivity** of what constitutes "quality"
- Tension between **coverage vs. tone vs. conciseness**
- The need to **collect rationales**, not just binary preferences

Such disagreements, if unaddressed, can introduce label noise into reward models. Some `RLHF` teams now *augment ratings with written justifications* and use `LLMs` to generalize from high-quality human reasoning, not just scalar labels.
