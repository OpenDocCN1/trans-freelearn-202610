## Modifications Needed to Adapt PPO for RLHF

While PPO is the default algorithm to consider when working with RLHF policy optimization, there are some challenges:

*   **Addressing Distribution Shift**: Since the reward model was trained on past policy rollouts or human-labeled data, it may encounter distributional shifts as the policy evolves. This necessitates periodic re-training or fine-tuning of the reward model using up-to-date trajectories sampled from the latest policy to maintain alignment with human intent. Techniques such as reward model ensembling or uncertainty estimation can also mitigate overfitting to outdated preferences.

*   **Regularization and Safety Constraints**: To prevent unintended policy behaviors, constraints can be incorporated during optimization. Some of these are already considered as part of PPO implementation. For example:
    *   `Entropy Regularization` encourages exploration to prevent premature convergence.
    *   `KL Divergence Constraints` ensure policy updates do not diverge too far from prior policies to maintain stability.
    *   `Reward Model uncertainty handling` by penalizing high-uncertainty reward predictions to avoid over-reliance on unreliable signals can also help.

*   **Evaluation and Iterative Refinement**: Evaluation of the policy trained with inferred rewards is an important step. These evaluations involve monitoring quantitative metrics such as reward accumulation and success rate. Similarly, qualitative assessments in the form of human evaluations and preference comparisons can be incorporated. Based on these evaluations, adjustments to the reward model or fine-tuning steps can enhance alignment and stability.

By integrating inferred rewards into policy optimization, RLHF enables policies to better align with human preferences, facilitating more interpretable and effective reinforcement learning outcomes.

## RLHF for Natural Language Applications

PPO algorithm steps discussed in the previous section require collecting roll-out trajectories. In natural language applications such as chat and dialog generation, the concept of a trajectory is adapted to suit natural language interactions. In this context, a `roll-out` refers to a generated sequence—typically a model response—produced in response to a prompt or user message. Instead of the typical sequence of states and actions seen in classical reinforcement learning, the "trajectory" corresponds to a structured conversation or a prompt-response pair, where each token can be viewed as an action taken by the policy. There are two possible ways to frame the term "trajectory" based on model interaction scenario:

### Full Conversation as a Trajectory

In some implementations, an entire conversation thread (e.g., multiple turns of user queries and model responses) is treated as a trajectory. The model generates multiple responses sequentially, forming a trajectory that is then evaluated with a reward model or human feedback.

### Single Question-Answer (QA) Pair as a Trajectory

In a more simplified version, each individual question-response pair is treated as a separate "episode," and a reward is assigned based on its quality.

The choice between these depends on the granularity of the feedback and how the reward model is trained. Next, we will consider how reward and feedback is considered for these trajectories.

### Reward and Feedback

The data structure of reward and feedback from human preferences is determined during reward model training. In a multi-turn conversation, the reward can be collected **per-turn** if each response is evaluated separately. Some approaches involve assigning a single reward to the entire conversation, and this can consider metrics such as coherence, helpfulness, and consistency across turns. More commonly, a preference-based reward is used, where a reward model is trained based on human preferences (e.g., ranking multiple responses). This model then assigns scores to responses during RL training.

### Training Process

The training process involves specific steps. First, the language model generates responses either as full conversations or question–answer (QA) pairs. These responses are assigned rewards based on the learned reward model incorporating human preferences. Then, the PPO algorithm optimizes the foundational model using this reward using these steps:

1.  **Collecting trajectories** (either conversations or QA pairs): The model generates outputs by sampling from its current policy. Each output is treated as a trajectory, corresponding to either a full multi-turn interaction or a single QA pair, depending on the framing used. These samples serve as the basis for reward evaluation and learning.

2.  **Estimating advantage functions using a baseline**: Each trajectory is scored by the reward model, which reflects human preference data. To determine how much the model should reinforce or discourage a given output, we compute the `advantage`—the difference between the actual reward and a baseline (often a value function). This helps focus learning on better-than-expected outputs while mitigating variance in policy gradients.

3.  **Perform policy updates with a KL constraint**: The policy is updated using the PPO objective to increase the probability of high-reward outputs. Crucially, PPO includes a `KL divergence` penalty that limits how much the updated policy can diverge from the reference policy (usually the supervised fine-tuned model). This constraint preserves the fluency and coherence learned during earlier fine-tuning stages while encouraging alignment with human preferences.

### Challenges in PPO for Chat Applications

The reward model consistency is a challenge as conversational quality is subjective and so are collected human preferences. The collection process should therefore be conducted using the best practices, as discussed in Chapter 7, and these are still evolving due to the nascency of the technology. Chat conversations can get long, and longer conversations introduce instability in training, making per-turn rewards more practical in some cases. Unlike usual RL environments such as games and simulations, exploring diverse responses from human conversational data and maintaining quality can be challenging. Therefore, generally, in practice, chat models typically use question-response pairs with preference-based ranking, rather than full multi-turn conversations, to stabilize training and make reward modeling more tractable.
