The plot shows that total reward does not progress until around 300 episodes. This indicates that the reward assigned by OpenAI gym for car climbing the mountain and reaching the flag is sparse, and only happens at the end of the episode. During iterations, the agent does not receive high reward until it climbs and reaches the goal. The replay buffer collects experiences, and these experiences help learn from good experiences with high rewards. We could shape the reward to improve training, but this is not always possible for every problem, without understanding the problem in depth. This is where human subject matter expertise plays a role. Since we are focusing on algorithmic aspects of RL in this chapter, and human reward models in general for this book, we will rely on OpenAI gym rewards. The example of mountain car with continuous states and discrete actions shows how deep neural networks can enable scaling of value-based learning to more complex or large state space problems. The `DQN` algorithm does not directly train the policy, but does it indirectly by first training the `Q` network. The algorithms considered here train the value function or action value function (the `Q` network) first using data stored in a replay buffer and then update the target network separately instead of using data from current policy directly, and therefore represent the class of off-policy algorithms. Notice that the actions output is still discrete, and `DQN` while allowing large or continuous state spaces, does not support continuous actions. There are some modified algorithms that train value functions and can be used for outputting continuous actions. Some of these are deep deterministic policy gradients (`DDPG`) and more scalable distributed distributional deep deterministic policy gradient (`D4PG`). If you are interested in diving deeper, please refer [`https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/user/algorithms.xhtml`](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/user/algorithms.xhtml) for understanding core algorithms from reliability and sample efficiency perspectives, and for further details on distribution and scaling refer to [`https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00979`](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00979). While these algorithms are remarkably sample efficient (requiring lesser number of samples to train) and solve the problem, they can suffer from three outstanding issues:

*   stability issues when updating from `Q`-network to target network,
*   value function overestimation of the expected return due to the `max` operator in the update rule (in case of `Q` function),
*   distribution mismatch between the policy used to collect data and the policy being updated.

Our general focus here is on `RLHF` and reward modeling based approaches, and there is no guarantee on stationarity of rewards as discussed in the paper Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences: [`https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03741`](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03741). For such cases on non-stationary rewards, instability of algorithms can be an issue. The problem of instability coming from reward model for off-policy algorithms is addressed to some extent by relabeling all the agent’s past experiences every update of the reward model, as presented in these papers such as `PEBBLE` [`https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.05091`](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.05091), and [`https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06521`](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06521). However, considerations of scalability to complex environments, overhead of unsupervised pretraining, and quality of relabeling cannot be ignored. On-policy algorithms are relatively more stable, have shown more promise in past works such as Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (`GAIL`): [`https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.03476`](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.03476) and have empirically proven to be effective in practice, for example `ChatGPT` and several other products made their `PPO` implementation a state of the art in `RLHF`. In the next section, we will introduce some on-policy algorithms that accommodate using continuous actions. If you are interested in more `RL` implementations, check out the repositories such as OpenAI Baselines [`https://github.com/openai/baselines/tree/master`](https://github.com/openai/baselines/tree/master) and Stable Baselines3 [`https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/`](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/) for high quality implementations, structured with breadth in mind, and CleanRL [`https://docs.cleanrl.dev/`](https://docs.cleanrl.dev/) for single file implementations structured to help readers to go through implementation details.

## Policy Gradient Methods

In policy gradient methods, the algorithm directly optimizes the policy based on estimation of the gradient of the expected reward with respect to parameters defining the policy. That means instead of generating policy from the value function as is the case of `Q`-learning, the policy is parametrized. If `π` represents the policy, as in previous chapters, we represent it as `π_θ`, where `θ` parametrizes the policy. The main idea is to adjust the policy parameters in the direction that maximizes the expected cumulative reward. The policy is often parameterized using a neural network. The policy represents the probability of taking action `a` given state `s` with parameters `θ`. The objective function is `J(θ)`, where `R` is cumulative reward obtained by following the policy `π_θ`, and objective is to maximize expected reward. The objective function can be written more explicitly for understanding better as the sum over all possible states and actions, weighted by their probabilities and the reward obtained. Here `P(s,a)` is the joint probability of being in state `s` and taking action `a`, and `R` is the total reward accumulated over an entire trajectory or episode. To maximize `J(θ)`, we need to compute its gradient with respect to the policy parameters `θ`. The likelihood ratio method helps in deriving this gradient. Gradient of the objective function with respect to `θ` can be represented as:

```
∇_θ J(θ) = E[∇_θ log π_θ(a|s) * R]
```

Notice that the gradient of such an objective function may not be easily tractable, and we have to use some mathematical tricks such as Log likelihood.

### Log Likelihood Method

From Calculus, we know that `∇_θ log f(θ) = ∇_θ f(θ) / f(θ)`. The logarithm of the probability function transforms the product of probabilities into a sum, which is easier to handle. So, we replace gradient as `∇_θ log π_θ(a|s)`. Another reason this helps is when gradients of small probabilities lead to numerical instability, logarithms prevent underflow, and only lead to negative numbers. It also makes computation of derivatives easier.

### Policy Update Rule

The computed gradients of policy are used to update the policy parameters using gradient ascent. The updated policy function can be updates of neural network weights, and output of continuous actions distribution. This is suitable for continuous action problems.
