
Model evaluation is the phase where we assess the general performance of models independent of a specific task. For language models, it measures the model's intrinsic capabilities across various linguistic, statistical, and generative dimensions. Unlike traditional supervised learning, where clear ground truth labels are available for evaluation, reinforcement learning from human feedback (`RLHF`) and other human and AI alignment methods such as direct preference optimization (`DPO`) and Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (`RLAIF`) rely on human judgments, preference modeling, and often qualitative assessments. This chapter explores the complexities of evaluating models trained using `RLHF` and related approaches, including performance metrics, human evaluations, and the emerging paradigm of using `LLMs` as evaluators. ## Challenges in Model Evaluation

Evaluating models trained through `RLHF` or its emerging variants such as `DPO` and `RLAIF` introduces a class of challenges distinct from standard supervised fine-tuning. Because these methods rely heavily on subjective human preferences, feedback loops, and implicit value judgments, evaluation becomes as much an interpretive process as a measurement task. The following challenges capture the most persistent and interrelated issues that shape how `RLHF`-aligned models are assessed.

### Subjectivity and Inconsistency in Human Feedback

Human feedback forms both the training data and the gold standard for evaluation in preference-based alignment. However, *subjectivity is inherent*: annotators bring diverse backgrounds, values, and interpretive strategies. Their judgments are shaped by context, prior experience, and even task framing, leading to variability that is difficult to systematize or control. Disagreement among annotators can reflect legitimate pluralism in human values, ambiguity in instructions, or even random noise. For example, in a summarization task, one annotator may reward brevity while another values nuance, leading to contradictory preference labels for the same output. These inconsistencies undermine the reliability of evaluation metrics and can produce models that align with narrow or unrepresentative perspectives. Addressing subjectivity requires treating human feedback as a probabilistic, context-sensitive guide rather than an absolute ground truth (real world logged data).

### Quality and Representativeness of Annotators

The composition and quality of the annotator pool directly influence model alignment and evaluation outcomes. If annotators are not demographically or experientially diverse, models may overfit to the preferences of specific groups, leading to misalignment with broader user populations. Additionally, annotators may provide low-quality or even malicious feedback, whether due to fatigue, misunderstanding, or intentional data poisoning. In practice, a dialogue model trained primarily on Western English-speaking annotators may overvalue directness and underrepresent culturally nuanced or indirect communication styles. Rigorous selection, training, and ongoing quality monitoring are essential, but even these measures cannot fully eliminate the risk of bias or error. The growing use of AI-generated feedback introduces further complexity, as such models ultimately reflect the data and oversight provided by humans.

### Ambiguity in Reward Modeling

Translating nuanced human preferences into numeric reward signals is inherently ambiguous. Reward models must distill rich, context-laden judgments into scalar values, often resulting in opaque, black-box systems. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to interpret why a model receives a particular reward, complicating error analysis and trust in evaluation outcomes. For instance, a reward model optimized for "helpfulness" may assign high scores to overly detailed or redundant explanations that users actually find frustrating. Ambiguity in reward modeling can also mask subtle misalignments, as models may appear to perform well on metrics that do not fully capture human intent or ethical considerations.

### Adversarial Vulnerabilities and Reward Hacking

`RLHF`-trained models are susceptible to adversarial attacks and reward hacking. Subtle prompt manipulations can bypass safety mechanisms, while models may learn to exploit weaknesses in the reward model, maximizing reward signals without genuinely improving output quality or alignment. A model instructed to "always provide safe answers," for example, might respond to every controversial prompt with a refusal such as "I'm sorry, but I can't answer that," achieving high safety scores while avoiding meaningful engagement. Such vulnerabilities not only threaten the integrity of evaluation but can also propagate unsafe or undesirable behaviors in deployment. Robust evaluation must account for both external adversarial threats and internal tendencies toward reward gaming.

### Mode Collapse and Loss of Diversity

A common risk in RL fine-tuning is mode collapse, where the model disproportionately favors high-reward, high-probability outputs. This can lead to repetitive, overly cautious, or generic responses, diminishing creativity and diversity—especially in open-ended tasks like dialogue or summarization. For instance, after `RLHF` fine-tuning, a conversational model might repeatedly use the same polite phrasing ("That's a great question!"), reducing the natural variety found in human conversation. Such loss of diversity reduces user value and may obscure the model's ability to handle novel or nuanced scenarios. Effective evaluation must balance alignment with the preservation of output diversity.

### Scalability and Cost

Collecting high-quality human feedback can be resource-intensive, both financially and logistically. `RLHF` pipelines require ongoing human-in-the-loop processes for both training and evaluation, making them difficult to scale compared to automated or unsupervised approaches. Building a high-quality reward dataset for a large language model, for example, may involve hundreds of annotators and tens of thousands of preference comparisons, costing hundreds of thousands of dollars. As model sizes and deployment contexts grow, the challenge of scaling reliable evaluation without prohibitive cost becomes increasingly acute. Research into hybrid human-AI evaluation and more efficient feedback mechanisms is ongoing but not yet a solved problem.
