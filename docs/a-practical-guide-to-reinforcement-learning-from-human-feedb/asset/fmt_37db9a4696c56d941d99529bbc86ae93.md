This final phase of `RLHF` is critical because it turns implicit human feedback into explicit optimization. Without it, models remain only partially aligned; with it, they become responsive to the values, goals, and safety constraints that matter in real-world deployment. Mastering this step is essential for building language models that are not only capable, but also trustworthy and aligned.

## Integration of Reward Models with Reinforcement Learning

Reward models (discussed in Chapter 7 in detail) are useful in simulating how humans score or give preferences when presented with example choices. Trained reward models can be used for improving policy optimization and aligning language models to human preferences. A high-level schematic of how reward models are connected with other components of `RLHF` is shown in *Figure 8.1*. A reward model outputs the signal (reward) or score on generated model response for given user prompts. During training, it uses human-annotated scores or preferences to compare its prediction for improving the model. Once trained, the model outputs rewards or scores for input texts. This reward is used to calculate how a policy model can be updated.

![Figure 8.1 Reward Model as part of optimizing the Policy Model in RLHF](media/file36.png)

*Figure 8.1 Reward Model as part of optimizing the Policy Model in RLHF*

In practical `RLHF` settings, generally the state-action pairs provided as input to the reward model can be **user prompts** and their **model-generated responses**. The user prompt can be questions, chat messages, images, or any other form of data, and the model may be asked to generate a text response, which can be a chat message or reply, an image, code, or simply question-answer pairs. The output from the reward model (reward) is used for updating policy model weights, and the update process is based on reinforcement learning. The reward model is already trained on large relevant samples of user prompts and example responses with human scores.

Unlike standard RL where a reward function calculates the reward based on observations or reward assignment for observations are explicitly mapped, `RLHF` relies on **predictions** of the reward model. This model is generally based on a neural network or a preference model such as the **Bradley-Terry model**. This means instead of explicit reward assignment, the predicted reward is an estimate of the desirability or quality of state-action pairs based on human feedback. In standard RL, the computed reward determines how much the weights of the policy model need to be updated. In this case, we use reward model inference instead to update the policy model. For applications such as those involving natural language, explicit reward assignment is not an option, and reward models are a practical alternative.

Reward models are based on training using human-annotated state-action pairs, which can be inconsistent, subjective, noisy, and depend upon the context. Generalization of reward inference to novel scenarios is a requirement and is often expected in practice. Fortunately, the uncertainty in learned rewards and generalizability can be dealt with in a similar way as we would handle any supervised learning model.

Predicted rewards from a reward model are usually normalized to make sure they are within an operating range. Usually, this is based on experimentation so that instability in policy optimization can be minimized. The output from reward models (the reward signals) can be used for training the policy model. Policy optimization can be experimented with any of the RL algorithms; however, on-policy algorithms, especially `PPO`, are preferred due to their relatively stable training. In the next section, we will discuss more on the `PPO` algorithm.

## PPO Algorithm for RLHF

We covered the `PPO` algorithm in Chapter 4 in depth. In this section, we will discuss it from the perspective of `RLHF`'s standard algorithm choice because `PPO` provides a good balance with respect to training stability and sample efficiency. Among on-policy algorithms, it has a simpler implementation of a clipping mechanism that attenuates policy updates from the previous iteration, thereby improving stability. While off-policy algorithms can be sample-efficient, `PPO` improves sample efficiency by reusing collected data for multiple updates, making it more sample-efficient compared to other policy gradient algorithms. Because of its balance between stability and efficiency, and its tested efficacy across a variety of environments, it is a default choice across RL applications including `RLHF`. Its wide adoption is also due to its relative stability and reliability in training, which is necessary for managing training of large systems with uncertainty.
