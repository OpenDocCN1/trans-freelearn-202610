
# Large Language Models and Reward Modeling

## Pretraining and Hallucination

Large Language Models (LLMs) are typically pretrained on large-scale corpora using supervised learning objectives such as `cross-entropy loss`, which optimizes the model to accurately predict the next token. This pretraining enables fluent and syntactically correct generation, but it does not guarantee that the outputs are factually accurate, helpful, or aligned with user intent. This disconnect often results in phenomena such as `hallucination`, where the model produces confident sounding but incorrect, unhelpful or misleading information.

## Supervised Fine-Tuning Limitations

To address such shortcomings, `SFT` is commonly employed to adapt models to task-specific datasets. While `SFT` improves task relevance and reduces some undesirable behaviors, it remains limited by the static nature of the training data and does not incorporate subjective human preferences. For instance, given two plausible responses, `SFT` offers no mechanism to indicate which is more helpful or aligned with human values.

## Reward Modeling in RLHF

`Reward modeling (RM)` addresses this limitation by learning a function that maps model outputs to scalar reward values, based on human feedback. The most common approach involves collecting comparison data, where annotators evaluate pairs of model responses to the same prompt and select the preferred one. These comparisons are used to train a reward model capable of generalizing human preferences to unseen outputs.

## Sequential Training of Reward Model and RL Agent

In the context of `RLHF`, the `RM` acts as a surrogate reward function to guide policy optimization. Typically, the `reward model` (`RM`) and the `reinforcement learning` (`RL`) agent are `not trained simultaneously`, but rather sequentially. First, the `RM` is trained on human preference data — usually in the form of output comparisons or ratings. Then, this fixed `RM` is used during `RL` to provide reward signals. Although online `RM` updates are occasionally explored in experimental settings, they are rare and require careful handling to maintain training stability. The modular, sequential structure of the `RLHF` pipeline remains a practical and widely adopted approach, as it enables easier maintenance, debugging, and independent iteration on the reward model.

## Emerging Online Reward Modeling Approaches

Emerging exceptions to fixed `RM` practice include systems like `Online Iterative RLHF`: `https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.07863`, which employ true online reward modeling: the `RM` is refined `during` policy optimization using proxy human feedback (e.g., `GPT-4` annotations) from newly sampled rollouts. Hybrid approaches, such as `HIRE` (Hybrid Intrinsic Reward: `https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12627v1`) or `RTV-GenRM`: `https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22230v1`, combine static preference-based rewards with dynamic components (e.g., curiosity-driven exploration or reasoning verifiers) to balance alignment and adaptability. While these advanced methods show promise, the sequential workflow remains dominant in production systems due to its simplicity, scalability, and acceptable resource feasibility.

## Preference-Based Reward Modeling

Preference-based `RM` is the dominant approach in `RLHF` for language models, particularly due to the open-ended nature of language generation, where multiple valid outputs may exist. In such cases, direct comparisons or scalar ratings from humans help resolve ambiguity more effectively than demonstration-based methods such as `imitation learning`, `behavioral cloning`, or `inverse reinforcement learning`. These alternative approaches focus on mimicking human behavior or inferring reward functions from demonstrations but are less suited to subjective tasks involving nuanced judgments.

## Reward Modeling Process

The reward modeling process typically involves three stages: (1) `Data Collection` — assembling input-output pairs and annotating them with human preference signals, such as binary comparisons (e.g., accepted vs. rejected responses) or numerical scores; (2) `Data Preprocessing` — structuring, cleaning, and validating the dataset to ensure quality and consistency; and (3) `Model Training and Evaluation` — training a neural network to predict reward values from the input-output pairs and evaluating its performance on benchmark datasets.

## Custom vs. Standardized Implementations

Reward modeling for LLM fine-tuning can be implemented using custom pipelines or prebuilt libraries. In `Chapter 3`, we explored how to build a reward model in `PyTorch` and integrate it into an `RL` setup for tasks such as gridworld navigation. In `Chapter 4`, we implemented a custom `PPO` algorithm for policy training. While such custom implementations offer transparency and flexibility, they also introduce significant maintenance overhead, particularly given the rapid pace of updates in model architectures and infrastructure.

## Hugging Face TRL Library

To mitigate these challenges, standardized libraries such as `Hugging Face`’s `TRL` provide integrated support for `RLHF` workflows. `TRL` facilitates the use of pretrained transformer models (e.g., `GPT`, `T5`, `BERT`), supports reward modeling, and offers out-of-the-box implementations of policy optimization methods such as `proximal policy optimization` (`PPO`). It also enables experimentation with newer approaches like `Direct Preference Optimization` (`DPO`).

## Benefits of Standardized RLHF Stack

For most practical use cases, the benefits of adopting a standardized `RLHF` stack — faster development, improved scalability, and long-term maintainability — generally outweigh the advantages of building and maintaining custom reward modeling infrastructure. In the next section, we examine the first step of reward modeling in detail: preference data collection and preprocessing.

## Data Preprocessing and Preference Data Collection

For collecting human preference data, usually crowd workers are tasked with chat sessions for interacting with models and adding preferences. This involves firstly asking the model questions or discussing some topic inducing a conversation, and upon receiving response from the model, rating the model outputs or assigning a preference score. This can be an expensive process, requiring human workers to perform these tasks.

The feedback or preference score can be inaccurate or biased, and based entirely on the crowd worker. Therefore, crowd worker curation becomes part of the data collection process, in addition to the data collection.
