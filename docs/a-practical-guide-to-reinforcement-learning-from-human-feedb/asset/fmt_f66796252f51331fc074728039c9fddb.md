
# Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback

While training a reinforcement learning agent to achieve a particular objective, the agent must undergo a vast number of iterations until it converges to a high reward. This involves going through several trial-and-error state-action sequences or trajectories that would not be optimal. The number of such trajectories can be very high if the agent must navigate through complex environments. Collecting experience of low reward trajectories can turn out to be costly.

The challenges encountered by RL agents, such as navigating complex environments and undergoing numerous trial-and-error iterations, underscore the need for more efficient learning strategies. The integration of human feedback offers a promising solution to enhance learning efficiency. For example, in training autonomous vehicles, human feedback can help the agent quickly learn traffic rules, safe driving behaviors, and respond appropriately to various road conditions, instead of going through every condition that requires avoiding these scenarios.

Human feedback can also help in overcoming exploration-exploitation trade-offs. By providing hints or nudges towards promising actions or states, humans can guide the agent to explore more efficiently, focusing on regions of the state-action space that are more likely to yield valuable information.

Incorporating human feedback allows RL agents to learn from expert demonstrations or preferences, enabling them to make more informed decisions. For example, in healthcare settings, RL algorithms can learn treatment policies by incorporating feedback from medical professionals regarding the effectiveness and safety of different interventions. Thus, rather than spending several training cycles figuring out valuable trajectories, using human expert guidance can save time by simply specifying human preferences or intention.

In scenarios where simulators or models are imperfect representations of the real world, human feedback can help bridge the gap between simulation and reality, leading to faster convergence and improved generalization.

## Grid World Example

Let's revisit the reinforcement learning problem where an agent needs to learn to navigate a grid world to reach a goal state while avoiding obstacles. In the example from the previous chapter, the agent receives a reward of `+10` upon reaching the goal state, a reward of `-5` upon hitting an obstacle, and `-1` for every step taken in order to penalize taking too long to reach the goal. The goal is to learn a policy that maximizes cumulative rewards over time.

The specific assignment of these rewards in the grid space is reward design, but not a form of human feedback as during training no human input is provided. Without human feedback, the agent might use exploration strategies like `epsilon-greedy` to randomly explore the grid world, gradually learning which actions lead to higher rewards through trial and error. For smaller state spaces, this may not appear to be a problem; however, this approach can be slow and inefficient in large state spaces.

Now, let's incorporate human feedback into the learning process. The human supervisor can observe the agent's actions and provide feedback in the form of preferences or evaluations of the agent's behavior. In the grid world example from the previous chapter, the human evaluates or provides binary feedback indicating whether each action taken by the agent was good (`+1`) or bad (`-1`). This is captured for a series of grid cells, based on its current state and resulting next state based on action taken. This type of human feedback is **evaluative feedback**, as the goal is known clearly and objectively, and direct feedback is possible.

## Mathematical Formulation

If we model the RL problem as a Markov decision process, where current state `s` and action taken `a` lead to next state `s'`, such that transition probabilities of next state depend only on current state and action taken `P(s'|s,a)`, and rewards are assigned for each of these sequences `R(s,a,s')`. The goal of the agent is to maximize cumulative returns, which is the action value function `Q(s,a)`. If `rt` is reward at time `t`, then cumulative reward or total return discounted over future rewards by `γ` is:

```
Gt = Σ γ^k * r_{t+k+1}
```

The way we incorporate human feedback based reward is by adding human feedback to environmental reward. The augmented reward function can be defined as `R*(s,a,s') = R(s,a,s') + H(s,a,s')`, where `H(s,a,s')` represents the human feedback for the action `a` taken in state `s` leading to next state `s'`. This feedback can be utilized to guide the agent's learning process by reinforcing actions that lead to positive outcomes and discouraging actions that lead to negative outcomes, ultimately accelerating the learning process and improving the agent's decision-making abilities.

## Types of Human Feedback

There are some other ways to integrate human feedback depending upon the scenario:

*   **State-Action feedback** `H(s,a)`: This form of feedback provides information about the quality of actions directly in the current state. It doesn't consider the specific outcome or next state resulting from the action. It's useful when the quality of the action can be evaluated without observing its consequences.

*   **State feedback** `H(s)`: Human feedback can also be provided at the state level, indicating the desirability or appropriateness of the current state. This form of feedback can guide exploration by directing the agent towards states that are more likely to lead to desirable outcomes.

*   **Cumulative feedback** `H(τ)`: In some cases, human feedback may be provided over entire trajectories (`τ`), capturing the overall quality of the agent's behavior over a sequence of actions and states. This type of feedback can be useful for evaluating long-term performance and encouraging consistency or convergence towards desired behaviors. This is especially helpful when the environment is complex and we want to limit human feedback to trajectories rather than asking explicit feedback over a large state-action space.

*   **Preference feedback** `H(a1>a2)`: Instead of providing explicit rewards or evaluations, human feedback can be in the form of preferences or comparisons between pairs of actions. This type of feedback is common in preference-based reinforcement learning, where the agent learns from comparisons between expert demonstrations or actions.

Therefore, the human feedback approach implemented and discussed in the previous chapter is only one of the many ways in which human feedback can be integrated into reinforcement learning training for accelerating the learning process.
