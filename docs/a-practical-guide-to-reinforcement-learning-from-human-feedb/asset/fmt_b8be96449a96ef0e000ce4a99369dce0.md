
### Learning from Human Reward or Evaluative Reinforcement

This category involves direct human evaluative feedback impacting the agent's rewards or learning process. It includes scalar rewards; humans provide scalar feedback (positive or negative rewards) based on the agent's actions or performance. This direct feedback helps shape the agent's behavior over time. The feedback is usually binary; simplified evaluative feedback where humans indicate whether an action or outcome is good or bad, often represented as +1 (positive) or -1 (negative). ### Preference-Based RL

This category focuses on human feedback in the form of preferences rather than absolute rewards. Humans indicate their preferences between different actions or outcomes, guiding the agent's policy development. It usually requires:

*   **Pairwise Preferences:** Humans provide feedback by comparing pairs of trajectories or outcomes, indicating which one they prefer. The agent uses this preference information to learn a reward function or policy that aligns with human preferences. Given two robot motion trajectories that reach the end goal, one of the trajectories might be safer or less awkward than another. A human preference on this is much easier to learn than providing complex features in a reward function. Similarly, asking users to provide feedback on two movies A and B, about their preference is much easier than deducing it from usage.

*   **Ranking:** Extends pairwise preferences to ranking multiple trajectories or outcomes, providing more detailed preference information for the agent to learn from. Ranking robot trajectories based on human feedback are easier to incorporate compared to extracting complex features and inferring from them. Similarly, direct feedback in the form of user rating and ranking simplify the objectives and reduce the possibility of errors due to developing complex reward functions accommodating all possible trajectories or outcomes.

Preference-based RL usually involves using preferences to directly optimize policy as in *Reward-rational (implicit) choice: A unifying formalism for reward learning*: [https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/2f10c1578a0706e06b6d7db6f0b4a6af-Abstract.xhtml](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/2f10c1578a0706e06b6d7db6f0b4a6af-Abstract.xhtml) and *A Survey of Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning Methods* (jmlr.org): https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-634.xhtml, but can sometimes involve reward function modification as can be seen in Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/d5e2c0adad503c91f91df240d0cd4e49-Abstract.xhtml.

### Human Feedback for Defining Reward Models

In this category, human feedback is used to construct or refine the reward model that the RL agent aims to optimize, as detailed in A Survey of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: [https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14925). It requires:

*   **Reward Model Training:** Humans provide feedback on the quality of outcomes or trajectories, which is used to train a reward model. This model then guides the agent's learning process. In training a self-driving car, human experts might review and rate different driving scenarios, such as lane changes or braking decisions. Their feedback on these scenarios helps build a reward model that informs the car's driving policy to ensure safety and adherence to traffic laws.

*   **Interactive Reward Specification:** Humans iteratively provide feedback to refine the reward model, ensuring it aligns with desired objectives and values. This can include corrections or adjustments based on observed agent behavior. In developing an AI for a video game, developers might initially set up a reward model based on generic goals like winning levels or scoring high. As the AI plays the game, human feedback helps tweak the reward model to better capture subtleties like strategic play or player enjoyment, ensuring that the AI's behavior matches the intended game experience.

*   **Direct Reward Feedback:** Humans provide explicit feedback on the agent's performance in terms of desired outcomes, and this feedback is used to construct a reward model. In training a chatbot, human evaluators might review interactions and provide feedback on how well the bot handles customer queries. This feedback directly influences the reward model, helping the bot learn to respond more effectively and provide satisfactory customer service.

This type of feedback and methodology is more scalable, and we will focus on more, in general.

### Semi-Supervised RL with Absolute Feedback on a Subset of Experiences

This approach combines elements of supervised learning with RL and is detailed in Semi-supervised reinforcement learning | by Paul Christiano | AI Alignment (ai-alignment.com): https://ai-alignment.com/semi-supervised-reinforcement-learning-cf7d5375197f, where humans provide absolute feedback on a subset of the agent's experiences:

*   **Labeling Experiences:** Humans label specific experiences (state-action pairs) with absolute feedback, such as correct or incorrect actions, which the agent uses to learn more efficiently. For a robotic arm trained to sort objects into different bins, humans can review a subset of the arm's sorting actions (e.g., placing objects into bins) and label them as correct or incorrect based on whether the objects are sorted into the right bins.

*   **Bootstrapping with Partial Supervision:** The agent receives detailed feedback on a subset of its experiences and uses this information to bootstrap learning on unlabeled experiences through RL algorithms. In algorithmic trading, an RL agent might receive detailed feedback on specific trading actions (e.g., whether a trade was profitable or not). This feedback helps the agent understand the impact of various strategies and bootstrap learning for trades that were not explicitly labeled.

*   **Hybrid Approaches:** Combining absolute feedback with other forms of human feedback (like evaluative rewards or preferences) to enhance learning efficiency and effectiveness. An autonomous vehicle might receive absolute feedback on specific driving scenarios (e.g., "this lane change was safe") as well as evaluative feedback (e.g., "overall driving behavior is aggressive"). Combining these types of feedback helps the vehicle improve its driving policy more comprehensively.
