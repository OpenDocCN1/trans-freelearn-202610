
# Policy Training

Policy training is the process of improving or learning a policy—a strategy that an agent uses to decide on actions based on states in the environment. A policy is a map from environmental states to actions. This map, model or function can be deterministic as in every state returns or points to a specific action every single time, or it can be stochastic and may have some noise and randomness with high probability of certain action values. Policy training involves determining a policy with maximum cumulative reward achieved by the agent over time. This reward can be given by the reward model (discussed in *Chapter 3*). During the training, the agent will search various spaces and explore new scenarios by trying new actions and achieving rewards for them. This process of exploring novel scenarios is termed as exploration. The agent also chooses previous actions that it got higher rewards for, with certain probability. This is called exploitation of high reward actions. The reward model, trained on human feedback data across various state-action trajectories, generates rewards for trajectories produced by the policy. The major objective of training is `policy optimization`—adjusting the policy to maximize the expected reward as predicted by the reward model. This involves refining the policy based on the feedback-induced reward signal.

## Shortcomings of Q-learning

In previous chapters, we consistently used `Q-learning` for policy optimization, since it was sufficient for a problem such as grid world navigation. However, there are several shortcomings of this algorithm. Some of the shortcomings of `Q-learning` algorithm are:

*   **Scalability to large or Continuous State Space**: In environments with a large or continuous state space (e.g., robotic control, autonomous driving), the `Q-table` becomes infeasible due to its size. The algorithm would require too much memory and computational power to store and update the `Q-values`.

*   **Lack of generalization for complex environments**: `Q-learning` uses a discrete lookup table for `Q-values`, meaning it cannot generalize across similar states. Each state is treated independently, even if states are similar or share common features and can be generalized e.g., image-based inputs in a video game. This lack of generalization limits the algorithm's ability to efficiently learn in environments where similar states can be treated similarly. More advanced methods, like `deep Q-networks (DQN)`, which use function approximation, are required.

*   **Instability with function approximations**: When combined with function approximation (e.g., using neural networks), `Q-learning` can become unstable or even diverge due to the correlated updates to `Q-values`. This is particularly problematic in large or complex environments where using a `Q-table` is impractical.

*   **Difficulty in convergence for non-deterministic or stochastic environments**: In environments where actions can lead to different outcomes due to stochastic dynamics, `Q-learning` can struggle to converge to the optimal policy. The updates to the `Q-values` can become noisy, and the algorithm may not be able to accurately learn the value of each state-action pair.

We will now see a demonstration of how these shortcomings can be overcome, using a continuous state space example as a problem case, and `deep q networks` (`DQN`) algorithm. Then, we will continue to a continuous action version to further show necessity and implementations of algorithms more suitable for continuous action environments.

## Mountain Car Environment

To realize improvements over `Q-learning`, we have to work with a simulation environment that `Q-learning` is challenged to solve. Mountain Car environment is one of the classical control benchmark environments used in research, and available on the gymnasium library: <https://gymnasium.farama.org/api/env/>. Gymnasium offices a variety of simulation environments, but to minimize domain overhead and keep focus on algorithms aspect, we just focus on mountain car for this chapter. Environments in gym are standardized and offer a consistent interface for interacting with them. This will allow you to easily switch to other environments to experiment with. The mountain car environment simulates a car situated in a one-dimensional valley between two hills. The primary challenge is to build enough momentum to reach the top of the right hill, which requires skillful manipulation of the car's velocity and position.

We start with framing the environment in the state-action-reward framework now. Firstly, the state space is continuous, providing real-valued measurements of the car's position and velocity. Therefore, first observation is position of the car along the x-axis (range: -1.2 to 0.6 meters), and second observation is velocity of the car (range: -0.07 to 0.07 meters/second). The continuous space is represented by `Box` type in gym, where each dimension of the space can take on a range of real values. Space is defined as `Box([-1.2, -0.07], [0.6, 0.07], (2,), float32)`. This means:

*   **Lower Bound**: `[-1.2, -0.07]` (for position and velocity, respectively)
*   **Upper Bound**: `[0.6, 0.07]` (for position and velocity, respectively)
*   **Shape**: `(2,)` which indicates there are two continuous dimensions (position and velocity).

For the default mountain car example, the actions are discrete and deterministic, meaning that each action results in a fixed change in the system's state according to the defined transition dynamics. The action space is defined as `Discrete(3)`. This means:

*   **Number of Actions**: 3 discrete options (0, 1, 2)
*   **0**: Accelerate to the left
*   **1**: No acceleration
*   **2**: Accelerate to the right

For any action, the mountain car states transition with following dynamics:

```
velocity(t+1) = velocity(t) + (action - 1) * force - cos(3 * position(t)) * gravity
position(t+1) = position(t) + velocity(t+1)
```

where `force = 0.001 and gravity = 0.0025`. The collisions at either end are inelastic with the velocity set to 0 upon collision with the wall. The position is clipped to the range `[-1.2, 0.6]` and velocity is clipped to the range `[-0.07, 0.07]`. Note that at the state transitions, the sample time `t` is such that each time step is 1 unit. Now that we understand how the mountain car is modeled, how it computes the states, and what the states and action ranges are used by the simulation, we will proceed to discuss the algorithms, starting with Deep Q Networks.

## Deep Q Networks

To overcome limitations of `Q-learning`, Deep Q-Network (`DQN`) was introduced as a significant advancement in reinforcement learning. `DQN` combines the strengths of `Q-learning` with the powerful function approximation capabilities of deep neural networks, allowing it to scale to complex environments that were previously intractable with traditional methods.

### Function Approximation

Instead of using a `Q-table`, `DQN` relies on a deep neural network model to approximate the `Q-value` function. Input to the network model are current state and outputs are `Q-values` for all possible actions. This approach enables `DQN` to handle large and continuous state spaces by leveraging the generalization capabilities of neural networks.
